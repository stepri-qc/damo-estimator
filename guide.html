Start here

Nine steps from a blank estimate to something you can put in a proposal. The tool loads with a worked default, so you always have live numbers to react to.

Set the engagement

Term, client AI maturity, whether volumetrics exist, estate profile, AIOps in or out, Customer Region (IME/EU/APAC/NA), and engagement type. The first four choices alone select the scenario — and with it your contingency, team shape, AIOps timing and savings target.

Say whether it’s greenfield or brownfield

Built and run by us from day one, or an existing estate we’re taking over? Brownfield is the conservative default — it auto-seeds four confidence/risk sliders lower and adds a direct Y1 FTE uplift (default +20%, tapering to zero by Y3) on top, since taking over an unfamiliar system genuinely costs more in the first year than the framework’s sliders alone capture.

Pick the service mix

Click AMS, IMS and/or DMS at the top of Service towers to say what you are proposing — any combination works. Use + Add another tower only when one line needs more than one tower, such as separate estates on different SLAs. Then set who provides L1, L2 and L3 for each.

Enter demand

Incident history if you have it — incidents with a severity mix, plus service requests and change requests, each with its own Low/Medium/High complexity mix (see Incidents, SRs & CRs). If you have no history, switch the tower to Complexity (apps by S/M/L/XL/XXL) or MAU. The tool handles all four scenarios, not just the ones with data.

Set SLAs and coverage

P1 response under 15 minutes adds 25% to L2/L3 staffing. Coverage window sets a headcount floor through the shift maths — often the binding constraint, not the ticket volume.

Set the estate complexity

Low, Medium or High — one deal-level call that sets the seniority pyramid, adds contingency as real FTE, and tilts the severity, SR and CR mixes toward the harder end. Leave it Not set for no contingency and a 1:2:4 pyramid. Then add any Special roles (L1.5, SRE, QA, Advisory) by hand. See Estate complexity.

Score confidence and risk

Nine drivers, nine risk categories. This is not decoration: the effective score sets your contingency posture and widens the Monte Carlo bands.

Choose the AIOps solutions and plan the transition

In AIOps efficiency calculator, tick the solutions this deal will actually apply and see the net FTE they save once the AIOps engineers are paid for. Decide how that freed capacity is used. Under Engagement, check the Transition phase weeks for knowledge transfer, shadow and reverse shadow.

Export and compare

Copy the auto-composed assumptions, exclusions, volume-band clause and risk log into the proposal annex, or use Export Excel for the bid team. Save versions under Scenarios to compare options side by side. Run Verify against framework in front of the client if you want to show your working.

Starting from an RFP

On a fresh visit the tool opens on an intake screen instead of the dashboard — add the RFP, an incident-history sheet, anything relevant, and let it pre-fill what it can before you touch the rail. Also reachable any time via Start from RFP in the masthead.

Add one or more documents

Paste text, or upload real files — .docx, .pdf, .xlsx, .txt, .md are all supported natively. Add a second, third document (the RFP, a separate incident-history workbook, an SLA sheet) and they combine into a single pass rather than needing to be merged by hand.

Detect inputs

A pattern-matching engine scans the combined text for contract term, which of AMS/IMS/DMS are mentioned, incident volume, P1–P4 SLAs and mix, coverage window, AIOps intent, MAU and tier ownership. Every match shows the exact text it came from — check it, do not just trust it.

Continue, or fix what is missing

Whatever was found pre-fills the estimator; whatever was not is simply left at default, editable in the rail exactly as normal. There is no separate gap-filling screen because the rail already is that screen.

This is pattern-matching, not comprehension. A published Artifact has no runtime AI access — there is no way for this page to actually read and understand a document the way a chat turn with Claude can. So Detect inputs is regex over known DAMO phrasing (“P1 response within 15 minutes”, “24x7 coverage”), not an AI summary. It will miss anything phrased unusually, and it will occasionally match the wrong sentence — that is exactly why every hit shows its source excerpt.

Reporting period — never assumed

Volumes in a document are usually a total over some period (“six-month manning data”, “FY25 incidents”), not a monthly figure. The AI extraction reads the stated period and converts to monthly; if the document states none, it must say so in the summary rather than quietly treating the numbers as monthly. You can also type Reporting period (months) on the intake screen — a value you enter always wins over anything inferred. Severity mix is always converted to genuine percentages of total incidents, and change requests are extracted as their own monthly figure.

Incident-history spreadsheets are read as tables, not prose

A cell reading P1, 20, 15, 120 does not match a sentence-shaped regex. Upload an incident-history workbook and the tool instead looks for a header row (a priority/severity column plus incident-count and response/resolution-SLA columns) and reads the data rows directly — aggregate volume, per-priority SLA in minutes, a derived mix. When both a spreadsheet and prose text mention the same field, the tabular figure wins: structured data beats a regex guess.

For a real AI summary: one click on Netlify, or hand it to Claude yourself

On damo-estimator.netlify.app, the Import Claude-prepared inputs panel has an Extract with AI now button. One click sends your document text to a real backend that calls Claude directly and writes the structured JSON straight into the box below — no copy-paste, no second chat window. It typically finishes in 10–15 seconds; a status line shows live progress and the token usage once done.

This is Netlify-only — a published Artifact has no way to call an external server, so on the Artifact (and everywhere else) the same panel instead offers Copy prompt for Claude. One click copies instructions, the target JSON schema and the document text you have entered into your clipboard — paste that into a fresh Claude conversation, and you get back a genuine narrative summary plus the same structured fields, written by an actual model reading the actual document. Paste the JSON it returns into the box below.

Either path ends the same way: hit Apply prepared inputs. The summary lands as a Document Summary card at the top of the dashboard, and flows into the exported register and the printed SA report.

The cascade — the one thing to get right

Tiering is a cascade, not a partition. This trips up most hand-built estimates, and the framework's own page-3 illustration settles it.

100 tickets arrive at L1. Only the 30 resolved there consume L1 effort. The 70 that pass through consume 3 hours each at L2 — all 70, including the ones that later escalate. Then 20% of those 70 escalate to L3 and consume 10 hours each on top of their L2 hours.

Why it matters commercially. Because L3 hours are additive and L3 ARE is 8–12 hours, a small shift in the escalation rate moves the estimate far more than a large shift in ticket volume. Drag L2 → L3 from 20% to 30% and watch. That is why the framework prices a higher Y1 escalation assumption as a risk mitigation, and why "reduce escalations" beats "reduce tickets" as an AIOps target.

Incidents, service requests & change requests

In History mode every tower carries three separate demand streams, each costed its own way. They are never blended into one pool.

  • Severity mix (P1–P4) takes counts or percentages and is normalised. It applies to incidents only — SRs and CRs never inherit it.
  • SR and CR complexity mixes are entered per tower (counts or %, normalised). Defaults: SR 60/30/10, CR 50/35/15. The charged ARE is the mix-weighted average — a 60/30/10 SR mix costs 0.45 h per SR.

  • Estate complexity tilts all three mixes toward the harder end (see Estate complexity); the effective mix is shown under each input.
  • L1 is the customer’s by default. New towers start with L1 = Customer and L2/L3 = us, so a fresh estimate has no L1 effort. Low SRs then fall to L2 rather than disappearing. Choose Full stack when L1 is in scope.

Why separate streams. SRs used to be folded into the incident pool and inherited its severity mix and L1/L2/L3 AREs, which inflated FTE badly for any tower with real SR volume. They have their own AREs and complexity now, and the incident mix never touches them.

AIOps break-even

Phasing. AIOps engineers are charged from the year deflection actually starts (Y2 in Scenarios 1 and 3), not from Y1 — a team with no deflection to show for it has no AIOps headcount. They follow the scenario ratio automatically; they are not a Special role.

An embedded AIOps engineer is added headcount. Below a threshold, adding AIOps makes the team bigger. Know where that threshold sits before you promise savings.

At a ratio of 1:r, AIOps adds 1/r to the team. The team only shrinks once deflection d satisfies (1 − d)(1 + 1/r) < 1, which solves to d > 1/(r + 1).

AIOps efficiency calculator

For the question a solution architect actually gets asked: if we apply these AIOps solutions, how much smaller does the team get, and what do we do with the capacity? Everything is in FTE, hours and person-months — still no rate card, no price.

Pick the solutions

The rail section lists all 14 AIOps use cases. Tick the ones this deal will apply. For each you can set Defl % (share of delivery effort it removes at full adoption), Start (year it goes live; 0 means the scenario’s own AIOps start year) and Ramp mo (months to reach full effect). Seed from O1 portfolio copies the optimiser’s funded set and start years; All and None do what they say.

Read the savings

The calculator card shows FTE freed, the AIOps engineers needed to build and run the tooling, and the net saving per year. Engineers are counted every year AIOps is in scope, from Y1 — before any solution is live — exactly as the headline estimate counts them, so a year with no deflection yet shows as a net cost. Per solution you see build effort, person-months saved over the term, payback month and hours freed per month.

Decide how to use the capacity

Split the net saving three ways: Reduce (a smaller run team), Redeploy (hours moved onto evolution or backlog work) and Commit (productivity promised to the client). The card compares the committed share with the scenario’s own savings target and flags any gap.

Optionally apply it to the estimate

By default the calculator is a what-if and the headline FTE is unchanged. Tick Apply to estimate and the deflection built from your chosen solutions replaces the scenario’s generic year-on-year curve everywhere — headline FTE, Monte Carlo bands, Traditional vs AI, staffing plan.

How solutions combine

Solutions stack multiplicatively, not additively: two solutions at 10% each remove 19%, not 20%, because the second only acts on what the first left behind. The combined figure is then capped at the tower automation ceiling (AMS 60%, IMS 70%, DMS 50%, volume-weighted on a mixed deal). A solution that deflects a tier we do not provide is greyed out — it buys us no headcount, the same rule the O1 portfolio uses.

A handful of small solutions will not clear break-even. At a 1:4 AIOps ratio the break-even is 20% deflection. The four prerequisite-free starters (knowledge retrieval, ticket classification, alert triage, summarisation) add up to about 12%, so on their own they make the team bigger. The default selection is the seven core L2 solutions, about 24% combined, which is close to Scenario 3’s own Y3 curve. On a very small team, even that can show a slightly negative net — one whole AIOps engineer is a large share of a 4-FTE team. That is a real result, not a bug: say so in the proposal.

Applying it can raise FTE in later years. The scenario curve compounds every year; a fixed set of solutions levels off once fully adopted. Wherever the solutions deflect less than the curve, applying them raises FTE — the card warns you, naming the years and the gap, before and after you apply.

The solution catalogue’s effort and deflection figures, and the MTTR figures used by the outcome model, are a proposed calibration and editable. They are the most valuable thing to replace with real engagement data.

Transition phase

The handover before service commencement — knowledge transfer, shadow, then reverse shadow — sized as its own block of effort.

PhaseDefault lengthTeam present
Knowledge transfer6 weeks (3 for greenfield)50% of the steady team + core
Shadow4 weeks80% of the steady team + core
Reverse shadow4 weeks100% of the steady team + core

The steady team is Year 1 delivery FTE with hypercare taken back out. The core (default 2 FTE) is the transition manager and leads, present throughout. A complex-legacy estate stretches every phase by 25%. All values are under Engagement and are a proposed calibration.

Transition is not hypercare, and it is not in Y1 FTE. Transition happens before service start, so the card reports it alongside the estimate as person-months, and the summary strip shows Transition PM. Hypercare is the first months of actually running the service and stays inside Y1 as an uplift, exactly as before.

Outcome-based model

What an outcome-linked commercial construct would look like around this estimate — expressed in effort, never money.

The commitment is the scenario’s own savings target (for example 12–15% a year in Scenario 3), compounded from the year AIOps starts and measured against the no-AIOps baseline. Measuring it against Year 1 instead would be misleading, because Year 1 is inflated by hypercare and the brownfield uplift. Around that target sit three bands:

  • Below the credit floor (default 80% of target): a service credit, scaling up to a cap (default 10% of that year’s effort).
  • Between floor and trigger: neutral.
  • Above the gain-share trigger (default 125% of target): we keep a share (default 50%) of the extra effort saved.

Each year shows the modelled AIOps deflection against these bands, with a status (BELOW FLOOR SHORT MEETS GAIN-SHARE), an indicative MTTR reduction from the selected solutions, and the tower SLAs carried into the KPI set.

The framework only recommends outcome pricing in Scenarios 2 and 4. In Scenarios 1 and 3 the card still shows the bands, but warns that they are a phase-2 option once volumetrics or AI maturity firm up. With the calculator in what-if mode, the modelled deflection equals the scenario curve, so the status will read MEETS by construction; apply the calculator to test a real solution portfolio against the target.

What changes downstream

  • Tiers we do not provide carry no effort, no headcount and no roles — but volume still cascades through them.
  • Team-shape splits renormalise over the tiers we do provide, so a "customer keeps L1" deal never staffs phantom L1 people.
  • AIOps use cases deflecting a tier we do not own drop out of the O1 portfolio. The client may benefit; they buy us no headcount.
  • The rostered coverage floor only applies where we hold a front-line tier. As the L3 escalation party only, coverage is on-call, not a rota.
  • Every adjacent tier pair under different ownership adds a coordination uplift to our ticketing effort.

You do not control your own inflow. If another party runs L1, their resolution rate sets your L2 volume. The estimate assumes the benchmark 70% pass-through — if the incumbent under-performs it, your volume rises with no contractual trigger. A volume-band clause will not protect you. Ask for a pass-through band as well.

They are not linked — if the band says “high contingency” and the level says 5%, say which you are using and why.

Seniority mix

Section Seniority mix (left rail, right after Service towers) sets the Lead : Senior : Consultant pyramid for L1–L3 from the estate complexity — Low 1:2:4, Medium 1:2:3, High 1:2:2, each editable; not set uses 1:2:4. It changes the grade split, never headcount. SDMs are graded wholly Lead; special roles keep 1:2:4; there is no Principal grade. Seniority uplift (default 0.25) layers on top: as the scenario’s shape moves pyramid → diamond → inverted, a share of Consultant shifts to Senior and of Senior to Lead.

The inverted pyramid is a grade story. As automation strips the Consultant-heavy base over the engagement years, the Lead share rises while headcount falls — the “leaner but senior-heavy team” narrative, visible in the Role & grade staffing plan.

Special roles

Section Special roles (right after Seniority mix): presets for L1.5 support, SRE, QA, Advisory and a Custom role. They apply to every tower, are entered by hand — one FTE number per role with an optional per-year override (blank years use the flat number) — and are added on top of Total FTE. They are not deflated by AIOps, not scaled by hypercare or the Brownfield uplift, carry no contingency, and sit outside the AIOps ratio. They are graded 1:2:4 and show in the staffing plan, location mix, register, print report and Excel.

What changed: the 35-role catalogue, role weights, the IMS “SRE is the objective” checkbox, the QA effort share and the Infrastructure Engineer role were removed. Old links that carry them still load; the values are ignored. AIOps engineers and governance are not special roles — they follow the scenario and the SDM rule.

SDM ratio is exactly 1:8, the manual-estimation convention, replacing the framework’s vaguer 1:8–10 range.

Structural complexity — IMS & DMS

A second complexity axis, independent of demand basis. Demand basis says how many tickets a tower will generate; structural complexity says how hard the estate itself is to run — more environments, hosting targets, databases and integrations mean more non-ticketing overhead, regardless of ticket volume. AMS is untouched — its existing incident-history/complexity-proxy inputs already cover it.

TowerInputs
IMSEnvironments supported, hostings supported, databases supported, non-production environments, and a “security-related aspects in scope” checkbox.
DMSData products supported, data platform built on (Databricks / Snowflake / Synapse / BigQuery / Redshift / Other), integrations, upstream systems, downstream systems.

What it does

Each tower's inputs feed a weighted score, banded into Low / Medium / High — shown live as a badge on the tower card. Two effects follow, both new, neither touching AMS:

  • Effort. The underlying score bumps the tower's B-factor (non-ticketing effort) continuously, not in three flat steps — the bump equals score × a per-point rate, calibrated so it lands on 10% right at the Low/Medium threshold and 20% right at Medium/High, then keeps climbing past that. A tower with 100 environments genuinely costs more than one with 10; it doesn't flatline the moment it crosses into High. Capped at a high sanity ceiling (default 200%) only against adversarial input. Stacks with the existing modern/legacy estate B-factor — a third lever alongside the SLA-stringency modifier (bumps L2/L3 hours) and the Brownfield uplift (bumps whole-tower FTE); none of the three double-count.
  • Roles. The tier badge no longer gates any role (the role catalogue was removed); it drives the B-factor bump and the demand proxy. A deal that needs an architect or security specialist adds one under Special roles.

The proposed defaults land at Medium on purpose. A freshly-added IMS or DMS tower's own field defaults score right in the middle of the Low/Medium/High bands — a deliberate “typical mid-size deal” anchor, not an artificially low or high starting point. Push the counts up or down to match the actual estate.

Every weight, cut-point and bump percentage here is editable in the Benchmarks panel, and is a proposed calibration — grounded in a supplied Data Managed Services deck's complexity-tier framing, not in [F]/[P].

Six traps

Things the tool surfaces that a hand-built estimate will miss.

1. Annexure 2 does not add up

The worked risk-drag example lists nine weighted values summing to 14.25, but the total row prints 15.3. The band conclusion (Medium) survives either way, so nothing downstream breaks — but if you reproduce the published total you will not match your own rows. The tool sums the rows.

2. Coverage usually beats demand

A 24×7 window at 2 per shift needs (21 × 2) / 5 = 8.4 FTE regardless of ticket volume. For small estates that floor is the entire estimate. When the tool flags cover, negotiating the support window will move the price far more than negotiating volume.

3. Effort-based sizing says you need almost no L1

Because L1 ARE is 15 minutes and is charged only on tickets resolved at L1, L1 is under 1% of resolution effort. The integer programme therefore allocates roughly none of the team to L1, while the pyramid convention allocates 45%. Both are defensible — L1 headcount is driven by coverage presence and triage throughput, not resolution hours. Size L1 from the roster, and state which basis the proposal uses.

4. Handing L1 to the customer saves almost nothing

On the default deal, full stack sizes at 23.7 FTE and "L1 by customer" sizes at 24.3 — it goes up. L1 is 0.7% of resolution effort at a 15-minute ARE charged only on resolved tickets, while the new ownership interface costs 4%. The real saving from moving L1 out is the rostered coverage, not the effort. If the commercial case for the split is headcount, drop FTE per shift too — otherwise the split buys you nothing and costs you an interface.

5. The automation ceiling caps the stretch curve

Per-tower automation ceilings (AMS 60%, IMS 70%, DMS 50%) are volume-weighted into a deal-level cap. On a mixed AMS+IMS+DMS deal that lands near 56%, so the 18%→65% template flattens rather than reaching 65%. The tool marks the year it binds. Raising a ceiling is fine — but only with evidence, because committing past it is how AIOps savings promises break.

6. Adding AIOps solutions does not automatically shrink the team

Every solution you tick raises deflection, but the AIOps engineers who build and run them are headcount too. At 1:4 you need more than 20% combined deflection before the team gets smaller at all. The calculator shows gross saving, AIOps engineers and net saving separately for exactly this reason — quote the net, and if it is negative in early years, call those years an investment.

Glossary

A / B factor
A is ticketing effort; B is non-ticketing effort (transformation, automation, CFR), expressed as a percentage of A.
ARE
Average Resolution Effort per tier. Framework benchmarks: L1 15 min, L2 2–4 h, L3 8–12 h.
SR / CR
Service request and change request. Each has its own Low/Medium/High mix per tower and its own ARE per level; neither inherits the incident severity mix.
Estate complexity
Deal-level Low/Medium/High call that sets the seniority pyramid, the contingency and a tilt of the severity, SR and CR mixes.
Special role
A manually entered role (L1.5, SRE, QA, Advisory, custom) added on top of Total FTE, outside the tower sizing.
Pass-through rate
Share of tickets escalating from one tier to the next. Improves year on year as runbooks, knowledge capture and automation mature.
FTE modifier (C)
Coverage adjustment from shift maths. Converts a support window into a minimum headcount.
POD
Team unit: Sustain (day-to-day ops), Transformation (SRE, AI, automation — 10–20% of effort), Flex (change requests), Governance.
Deflection
Share of demand removed by automation and AIOps, as a cumulative percentage against the Y1 baseline.
Automation ceiling
The maximum credible cumulative deflection for a tower type. Caps the productivity curve.
Risk drag
Annexure 2 overlay subtracted from the weighted driver score to give the effective confidence score.
Volume band clause
Contract term defining the ticket-volume range within which the fixed fee holds. Defaults to ±15% of modelled Y1 demand.
QUBO
Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation. A problem stated as minimising x'Qx over binary x, with constraints folded in as penalty terms. The native input format for quantum annealers and QAOA.
Transition
Knowledge transfer, shadow and reverse shadow before service start. Sized in person-months beside the estimate, never inside Y1 FTE.
Net FTE saved
FTE freed by the selected AIOps solutions, minus the AIOps engineers needed to build and run them.
Service credit / gain-share
Outcome-model bands around the savings target: a credit below the floor, a shared saving above the trigger. Expressed in person-months here, not money.
P80
The value 80% of Monte Carlo samples fall below. The number to commit to on a fixed fee, rather than the point estimate.
