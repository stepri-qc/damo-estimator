Start here

Six steps from a blank estimate to something you can put in a proposal. The tool loads with a worked default, so you always have live numbers to react to.

Set the engagement

Term, client AI maturity, whether volumetrics exist, estate profile, AIOps in or out, delivery region (IME/EU/APAC/NA), and engagement type. The first four choices alone select the scenario — and with it your contingency, team shape, AIOps timing and savings target.

Say whether it’s greenfield or brownfield

Built and run by us from day one, or an existing estate we’re taking over? Brownfield is the conservative default — it auto-seeds four confidence/risk sliders lower and adds a direct Y1 FTE uplift (default +20%, tapering to zero by Y3) on top, since taking over an unfamiliar system genuinely costs more in the first year than the framework’s sliders alone capture.

Pick the service mix

Click AMS, IMS and/or DMS at the top of Service towers to say what you are proposing — any combination works. Use + Add another tower only when one line needs more than one tower, such as separate estates on different SLAs. Then set who provides L1, L2 and L3 for each.

Enter demand

Incident history if you have it. If you do not, switch the tower to Complexity (apps by S/M/L/XL/XXL) or MAU. The tool handles all four scenarios, not just the ones with data.

Set SLAs and coverage

P1 response under 15 minutes adds 25% to L2/L3 staffing. Coverage window sets a headcount floor through the shift maths — often the binding constraint, not the ticket volume.

Score confidence and risk

Nine drivers, nine risk categories. This is not decoration: the effective score sets your contingency posture and widens the Monte Carlo bands.

Export the register

Copy the auto-composed assumptions, exclusions, volume-band clause and risk log straight into the proposal annex. Then run Verify against framework in front of the client if you want to show your working.

Starting from an RFP

On a fresh visit the tool opens on an intake screen instead of the dashboard — add the RFP, an incident-history sheet, anything relevant, and let it pre-fill what it can before you touch the rail. Also reachable any time via Start from RFP in the masthead.

Add one or more documents

Paste text, or upload real files — .docx, .pdf, .xlsx, .txt, .md are all supported natively. Add a second, third document (the RFP, a separate incident-history workbook, an SLA sheet) and they combine into a single pass rather than needing to be merged by hand.

Detect inputs

A pattern-matching engine scans the combined text for contract term, which of AMS/IMS/DMS are mentioned, incident volume, P1–P4 SLAs and mix, coverage window, AIOps intent, MAU and tier ownership. Every match shows the exact text it came from — check it, do not just trust it.

Continue, or fix what is missing

Whatever was found pre-fills the estimator; whatever was not is simply left at default, editable in the rail exactly as normal. There is no separate gap-filling screen because the rail already is that screen.

This is pattern-matching, not comprehension. A published Artifact has no runtime AI access — there is no way for this page to actually read and understand a document the way a chat turn with Claude can. So Detect inputs is regex over known DAMO phrasing (“P1 response within 15 minutes”, “24x7 coverage”), not an AI summary. It will miss anything phrased unusually, and it will occasionally match the wrong sentence — that is exactly why every hit shows its source excerpt.

Incident-history spreadsheets are read as tables, not prose

A cell reading P1, 20, 15, 120 does not match a sentence-shaped regex. Upload an incident-history workbook and the tool instead looks for a header row (a priority/severity column plus incident-count and response/resolution-SLA columns) and reads the data rows directly — aggregate volume, per-priority SLA in minutes, a derived mix. When both a spreadsheet and prose text mention the same field, the tabular figure wins: structured data beats a regex guess.

For a real AI summary: one click on Netlify, or hand it to Claude yourself

On damo-estimator.netlify.app, the Import Claude-prepared inputs panel has an Extract with AI now button. One click sends your document text to a real backend that calls Claude directly and writes the structured JSON straight into the box below — no copy-paste, no second chat window. It typically finishes in 10–15 seconds; a status line shows live progress and the token usage once done.

This is Netlify-only — a published Artifact has no way to call an external server, so on the Artifact (and everywhere else) the same panel instead offers Copy prompt for Claude. One click copies instructions, the target JSON schema and the document text you have entered into your clipboard — paste that into a fresh Claude conversation, and you get back a genuine narrative summary plus the same structured fields, written by an actual model reading the actual document. Paste the JSON it returns into the box below.

Either path ends the same way: hit Apply prepared inputs. The summary lands as a Document Summary card at the top of the dashboard, and flows into the exported register and the printed SA report.

The cascade — the one thing to get right

Tiering is a cascade, not a partition. This trips up most hand-built estimates, and the framework's own page-3 illustration settles it.

100 tickets arrive at L1. Only the 30 resolved there consume L1 effort. The 70 that pass through consume 3 hours each at L2 — all 70, including the ones that later escalate. Then 20% of those 70 escalate to L3 and consume 10 hours each on top of their L2 hours.

Why it matters commercially. Because L3 hours are additive and L3 ARE is 8–12 hours, a small shift in the escalation rate moves the estimate far more than a large shift in ticket volume. Drag L2 → L3 from 20% to 30% and watch. That is why the framework prices a higher Y1 escalation assumption as a risk mitigation, and why "reduce escalations" beats "reduce tickets" as an AIOps target.

AIOps break-even

An embedded AIOps engineer is added headcount. Below a threshold, adding AIOps makes the team bigger. Know where that threshold sits before you promise savings.

At a ratio of 1:r, AIOps adds 1/r to the team. The team only shrinks once deflection d satisfies (1 − d)(1 + 1/r) < 1, which solves to d > 1/(r + 1).

What changes downstream

  • Tiers we do not provide carry no effort, no headcount and no roles — but volume still cascades through them.
  • Team-shape splits renormalise over the tiers we do provide, so a "customer keeps L1" deal never staffs phantom L1 people.
  • AIOps use cases deflecting a tier we do not own drop out of the O1 portfolio. The client may benefit; they buy us no headcount.
  • The rostered coverage floor only applies where we hold a front-line tier. As the L3 escalation party only, coverage is on-call, not a rota.
  • Every adjacent tier pair under different ownership adds a coordination uplift to our ticketing effort.

You do not control your own inflow. If another party runs L1, their resolution rate sets your L2 volume. The estimate assumes the benchmark 70% pass-through — if the incumbent under-performs it, your volume rises with no contractual trigger. A volume-band clause will not protect you. Ask for a pass-through band as well.

Plus Machine Learning Engineer for AIOps, and Service Delivery Manager as the only governance role, present on every engagement — every other governance role (Delivery Principal, Solution Architect, Enterprise Architect, Data Architect, and the rest of the 37) is off by default.

For most tiers there's exactly one eligible candidate, so it automatically takes 100% of that tier's headcount. The full catalogue is still there in Roles & grades — switch on whatever a specific deal genuinely needs (a Data Scientist for an ML-heavy DMS engagement, say). Off-by-default is a starting posture, not a ceiling.

The inverted pyramid is a grade story. Every role splits Lead : Senior : Consultant at a proper pyramid ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 — no Principal grade, rarely used here. As automation strips the Consultant-heavy L1 base over the engagement, the Lead share of a shrinking team rises. The staffing card shows that movement year by year in your own ladder.

Service Delivery Manager is graded wholly Lead, not split — the one deliberate exception to the uniform 1:2:4 ratio, and it falls out for free since SDM is the sole role left in governance.

Two roles are genuinely gated in or out, not just down-weighted — if they're not eligible, they aren't candidates at all.

SRE can only be used with IMS. The restriction sits at the role's tower eligibility, not just the checkbox — even switching SRE on and setting the flag for an AMS or DMS tower produces zero SRE headcount there. That's why the checkbox itself only appears on IMS tower cards, and the evolution input only appears on AMS ones.

Data Engineer is the one role that spans both the L2 and L3 headcount pools for DMS, reflecting "we use Data Engineer for L2/L3" — every other on-by-default role draws from exactly one tier.

A mixed AMS+IMS deal loads both towers' roles independently and at the same time — SSE from the AMS slice, Infrastructure Support Engineer from the IMS slice — since each tower resolves against its own headcount share, not a deal-wide blend.

SDM ratio defaults to 1:8, the manual-estimation convention, replacing the framework's vaguer 1:8–10 range.

The split is uniform, not per-role. Every role's Year 1 FTE gets the same percentage split — a Developer at 20/30/50 lands at those same three shares, and so does everyone else. It does not pin specific roles to specific locations. A "Developers from Romania, everyone else from India" pattern is achieved by shaping the overall percentages to roughly match that mix, not by assigning Developer to nearshore directly. If you need true per-role location pinning, that's a follow-up, not something the current mix does.

Five traps

Things the tool surfaces that a hand-built estimate will miss.

1. Annexure 2 does not add up

The worked risk-drag example lists nine weighted values summing to 14.25, but the total row prints 15.3. The band conclusion (Medium) survives either way, so nothing downstream breaks — but if you reproduce the published total you will not match your own rows. The tool sums the rows.

2. Coverage usually beats demand

A 24×7 window at 2 per shift needs (21 × 2) / 5 = 8.4 FTE regardless of ticket volume. For small estates that floor is the entire estimate. When the tool flags cover, negotiating the support window will move the price far more than negotiating volume.

3. Effort-based sizing says you need almost no L1

Because L1 ARE is 15 minutes and is charged only on tickets resolved at L1, L1 is under 1% of resolution effort. The integer programme therefore allocates roughly none of the team to L1, while the pyramid convention allocates 45%. Both are defensible — L1 headcount is driven by coverage presence and triage throughput, not resolution hours. Size L1 from the roster, and state which basis the proposal uses.

4. Handing L1 to the customer saves almost nothing

On the default deal, full stack sizes at 23.7 FTE and "L1 by customer" sizes at 24.3 — it goes up. L1 is 0.7% of resolution effort at a 15-minute ARE charged only on resolved tickets, while the new ownership interface costs 4%. The real saving from moving L1 out is the rostered coverage, not the effort. If the commercial case for the split is headcount, drop FTE per shift too — otherwise the split buys you nothing and costs you an interface.

5. The automation ceiling caps the stretch curve

Per-tower automation ceilings (AMS 60%, IMS 70%, DMS 50%) are volume-weighted into a deal-level cap. On a mixed AMS+IMS+DMS deal that lands near 56%, so the 18%→65% template flattens rather than reaching 65%. The tool marks the year it binds. Raising a ceiling is fine — but only with evidence, because committing past it is how AIOps savings promises break.

Glossary

A / B factor
A is ticketing effort; B is non-ticketing effort (transformation, automation, CFR), expressed as a percentage of A.
ARE
Average Resolution Effort per tier. Framework benchmarks: L1 15 min, L2 2–4 h, L3 8–12 h.
Pass-through rate
Share of tickets escalating from one tier to the next. Improves year on year as runbooks, knowledge capture and automation mature.
FTE modifier (C)
Coverage adjustment from shift maths. Converts a support window into a minimum headcount.
POD
Team unit: Sustain (day-to-day ops), Transformation (SRE, AI, automation — 10–20% of effort), Flex (change requests), Governance.
Deflection
Share of demand removed by automation and AIOps, as a cumulative percentage against the Y1 baseline.
Automation ceiling
The maximum credible cumulative deflection for a tower type. Caps the productivity curve.
Risk drag
Annexure 2 overlay subtracted from the weighted driver score to give the effective confidence score.
Volume band clause
Contract term defining the ticket-volume range within which the fixed fee holds. Defaults to ±15% of modelled Y1 demand.
QUBO
Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimisation. A problem stated as minimising x'Qx over binary x, with constraints folded in as penalty terms. The native input format for quantum annealers and QAOA.
P80
The value 80% of Monte Carlo samples fall below. The number to commit to on a fixed fee, rather than the point estimate.
